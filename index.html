CONSIDERING CRYPTO?
Most crypto studies look at ownership, but this approach risks masking important differences between non-owning groups: those who would not consider owning crypto, and those who would consider owning but haven't acted yet.
Cryptocurrency is a rapidly expanding, multi-trillion dollar landscape of digital assets spanning sociopolitical, psychological, technological, and financial domains.

Understanding who considers and owns crypto (and which types people consider and own) matters for communicators, policymakers, educators, and researchers who need to understand people at each adoption stage.
Most research on cryptocurrency adoption focuses only on owners. Prior work that accounts for considerers relies on methods that don't allow for the direct comparison between rejecting, considering, and owning groups.

As a result, we know little about the broader patterns of individual-level crypto adoption in the United States.
According to the Theory of Planned Behavior (Ajzen 1985; 2020; Fishbein and Ajzen 1975), people must think about and form intentions to engage in a behavior before taking action.

However, thinking positively about a behavior doesn't always lead to intention, and intentions don't guarantee behavioral follow through.
We argue that considerers (i.e., non-owners who would consider owning crypto) and existing owners may both differ from rejectors (i.e., non-owners who would not consider owning crypto).

But considerers aren't necessarily like owners in all ways or to the same degree.

TESTING FOR POTENTIAL DIFFERENCES BETWEEN REJECTORS, CONSIDERERS, AND OWNERS
Identifying the three groups is one thing. Testing whether rejectors, considerers, and owners differ requires creating a single outcome variable that accounts for each behavioral stage.
MEASURES AND MODELING
We use a multivariate model that simultaneously estimates the probability of being in the rejecting, considering, or owning group. We repeat the analyses in three separate samples (all different people, points in time, and survey providers) to ensure the reliability of our results.
Multivariate, generalized ordered logistic regression (partial proportional odds) models — run separately for each of three independent, nationally-representative samples of US adults.

WHAT OUR FINDINGS MEAN FOR...
Our findings have significant implications for multiple interests and domains.
Understanding who considers, and what moves them from intentions to action, has direct implications for product design, onboarding, and communication strategies. Whether one's goal is to endorse or denounce crypto, the "considerer" is arguably the most actionable audience: potentially open to adoption, but hasn't taken action yet.

Our work suggests that no single approach will appeal to all. For example, efforts to reduce uncertainty and promote legitimacy may attract considerers, but backfire with owners who think differently about institutional disruption and risk.
That roughly 20% of US adults would consider owning cryptocurrencies suggests significant latent demand. However, consideration may depend on pending regulation, perceived risk, and other legitimacy signals, including endorsement from trusted elites. Policymakers in this area will likely need to balance both mainstream and alternative frames.
Looking only at ownership obscures variation among non-owners. By accounting for considerers, we find certain factors (e.g., ideology and tech attitudes) do matter for crypto adoption — just not for the ownership stage. Our approach also helps to determine when other factors (e.g., Need for Chaos) matter in the crypto adoption process, providing important direction for future research.
Considering and owning rates from all three studies suggest US crypto adoption is at or nearing the "early majority" stage. But we don't yet know how the crypto landscape will evolve, or whether the motivations of the next "wave" of adopters will look the same. Regardless, our findings align with decades of attitude-behavior research and should be broadly applicable even amid variation in how cryptocurrency is understood and/or used.
While most respondents in all three of our samples would consider or own Bitcoin, a sizable share are not concerned with Bitcoin at all. Others engage with multiple types. From a methodological perspective, this makes it virtually impossible to know whether an observed relationship is unique to Bitcoin or reflects a more general propensity toward cryptocurrency overall.

For example, younger individuals may be more likely to own Bitcoin. But if younger individuals also own Ethereum, Dogecoin, Fartcoin, and/or other cryptocurrencies, we can't conclude that age predicts Bitcoin ownership per se.

Even if one's primary interest is Bitcoin (or any specific asset), focusing only on that asset risks overlooking that many people treat "crypto" as a broader category rather than an isolated good. Understanding whether certain factors are asset-specific requires accounting for the myriad cryptocurrencies, then empirically testing (in the same model!) whether there are indeed differences across asset types.

We tested for, but did not ultimately find, asset-specific differences in our data. While it remains possible that Bitcoin (or other cryptocurrencies) could eventually have unique adoption patterns, our evidence indicates a broad orientation toward digital assets, in general, rather than a specific digital asset type.
